The Dick to Drama Ratio: and Other Impossible Math Equations

A first-person account. Content note: emotional abuse, threats, infidelity and physical violence.

When someone withholds the information you need, makes it impossible to meet their expectations, and then blames you for failing, the math was never meant to add up.

I will not cover this up. Not because I want to turn my private life into a spectacle, but because silence is one of the ways harmful behavior gets normalized. And because someone else may recognize the pattern before it becomes dangerous.

The impossible equation

At lunch, I asked when you would be discharged from the hospital. You said it could be a few more days. I missed a call while I was working, but you left no message explaining that the situation had changed. No discharge update. No request for a ride. No information that would have allowed me to change my plans.

So I planned to visit after work, as I had done through much of your admission. Between work and commuting, I was gone approximately fifteen hours that day. Before you were admitted, you had been staying at my home while I cared for you and urged you to see a doctor. You repeatedly refused treatment. Your sister and I were worried and exhausted, trying to help while you went silent or declined care.

When I learned from your sister—not from you—that you had been discharged, I checked my house and started driving toward the hospital. I had already left you my credit card and a note saying I could cover an Uber if you were discharged before I got home. All you had to do was let me know.

Halfway to the hospital, I was told someone was already coming to pick you up and that I did not need to continue. Then your friend arrived and was told you had taken a Lyft. You weren't at my house, and you weren't answering. Exhausted, I went home and fell asleep.

Just before midnight, you arrived banging on my door, accusing me of abandoning you at the hospital and stealing your truck. But we had already discussed moving your truck to my house, and you had given me the keys during my first hospital visit. I had been told you had left the hospital. I had tried to find you. I had offered transportation. I could not respond to information you refused to share.

You cannot refuse to communicate and then call someone uncaring because they could not read your mind.

When the story becomes a weapon

What followed was not just an argument about a ride. You admitted to cheating and tried to blame me for your choice. You insulted me. You said you would feel justified putting your hands on me. Then you hit me. My face was left swollen and bruised.

You took my car keys and drove away in my vehicle. Outside, near the neighbor's house, the insults and threats continued. After I got my keys back, you left in your truck before police arrived. Later I saw a Facebook account of events suggesting I had stolen that truck. A message from the neighbor made clear that he had heard a version in which I was the unreasonable one and you had been abandoned.

I cannot speak for what anyone else believed or intended. I can speak to what I experienced: the effort I made, the information I was given, the threats I heard, and the injury I sustained. I will not pretend that the violence did not happen simply because another story is more comfortable for someone else to tell.

The reaction is not the whole story

There is a pattern worth naming: one person creates confusion, withholds information, pushes and provokes, and then points to the other person's distressed response as if it explains everything. Sometimes this is called blame-shifting or reactive abuse, though the latter term can be misleading. A reaction does not automatically make every response appropriate. It also does not erase the conduct that preceded it.

Disagreements do not justify intimidation. Frustration does not justify threats. Infidelity is a choice, not something a partner causes by failing an impossible test. And no argument justifies hitting someone.

I can take responsibility for my own words and reactions without accepting responsibility for someone else's violence. Both things can be true.

Provoking someone into a reaction is not okay. Using that reaction to erase your own behavior is not accountability. And physical violence is never an acceptable answer.

The part I refuse to hide

I showed up. I cared for you. I tried to get you help. I visited. I offered transportation. I tried to find you. I was exhausted, and I made decisions based on the information I actually had—not the information you never gave me.

I did not deserve to be threatened or hit. I do not have to conceal my bruises to protect a version of events that makes someone else look better.

Love is literally the whole point. But love does not mean accepting violence, surrendering the truth, or making yourself smaller to keep someone else from being accountable.

For anyone who recognizes this

If any part of this feels familiar, you do not have to wait for a situation to become worse before asking for help. You can talk confidentially with an advocate about safety planning, documenting incidents, housing, legal options, or simply understanding what you are experiencing. You do not have to decide whether to leave before reaching out.

Support and safety resources (United States)

Immediate danger: Call 911 or your local emergency number if it is safe to do so.

National Domestic Violence Hotline: Call 800-799-SAFE (7233), text START to 88788, or visit thehotline.org for confidential support and safety planning.

Florida domestic violence support: Visit the Florida Coalition Against Domestic Violence website for information about services; for immediate referrals, use the National Domestic Violence Hotline above.

Documenting injuries: Consider seeking medical care, photographing injuries, saving messages and threats, and storing records somewhere the other person cannot access. An advocate can help you consider safe next steps.

Digital safety: If someone monitors your device or accounts, consider using a trusted device to contact support. Browsing history and messages may be visible to someone with access to your phone.

This essay describes the author's personal experience and perspective. It is not a legal finding or a diagnosis. Resource availability may change.

Share this with someone who needs to hear it: Accountability starts with telling the whole story—not just the part that makes someone else's reaction look unreasonable.
